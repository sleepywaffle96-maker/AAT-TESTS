Unit: Credit Management - Chapter 8 Theory

Lesson 8.1: The Credit Control Function & Credit Risk Assessment

In corporate liquidity ecosystems, granting credit acts as an essential catalyst for maximizing sales volume. However, poor oversight of accounts receivable damages cash flow and can trigger unmanageable bad debt write-offs. Under the Level 4 Credit Management (CRMN) syllabus, the credit control function maintains an ongoing equilibrium between boosting revenue and minimizing default risk.

To evaluate potential or existing credit accounts, credit managers rely on quantitative and qualitative risk assessments:

  • The 5 Cs of Credit: A foundational framework consisting of Character (reputation, history), Capacity (cash flow ability to clear debts), Capital (financial asset base), Collateral (assets pledged as security shields), and Conditions (macroeconomic environment factors).
  • Credit Scoring Models: Mathematical systems that pull financial data (liquidity ratios, credit agency reports) and assign a numerical score to a applicant to determine credit worthiness and set safe credit limits.
  • Information Sources: External indicators include credit reference agency database reports (e.g., Experian), bank trade references, and public accounts published via Companies House registries.

Lesson 8.2: Monitoring Receivables, Performance Metrics & Overdue Balances

Once credit lines are granted, the credit department must systematically monitor outstanding balances. The main analytical tool for tracking payment default risk is the Aged Debtors Ledger Analysis, which breaks down total accounts receivable into chronological payment blocks (e.g., Current, 30 days overdue, 60 days overdue, 90+ days overdue).

To measure departmental performance and working capital velocity, advanced controllers track specialized financial ratios:

CREDIT MANAGEMENT ANALYTICAL RATIO CODES:
• Debtor Days (DSO) = (Trade Receivables / Gross Credit Sales) × 365
• Collection Efficiency Ratio = (Actual Cash Collections / Target Overdue Debt) × 100
• Bad Debt Ratio = (Total Bad Debt Write-offs / Gross Credit Sales) × 100

A high Debtor Days metric or Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) trajectory signals that cash remains locked inside the sales ledger, increasing company interest overheads on bank overdrafts and raising liquidity default risks.

Lesson 8.3: Debt Collection Techniques, Factoring & Legal Collection Frameworks

When accounts cross overdue thresholds, the credit controller implements progressive debt collection procedures. Communication steps must follow a strict, structured timeline to preserve customer business relationships while securing corporate cash flows.

The primary external debt optimization and collection mechanics include:

  • Debt Factoring: Selling the accounts receivable ledger to a third-party specialist company (the factor) for an immediate cash advance (standardly 80%-90% of book value). Under With Recourse factoring, the company bears the risk of bad debts; under Without Recourse factoring, the factor absorbs all bad debt losses.
  • Invoice Discounting: Drawing confidential short-term loans against the value of outstanding sales invoices. The business retains internal administration of the credit ledger and hides the arrangement from clients.
  • Legal Remedies: Comprises issuing formal Letters Before Action (LBA), submitting statutory late payment interest surcharges under UK commercial legislation, escalating cases to the County Court, or initiating statutory winding-up petitions for severe corporate insolvencies.
"The choice of collection technique must incorporate legal compliance and cost proportionality metrics. Taking a long-term customer to court over a minor, unconfirmed balance tracking dispute represents process failure, as legal fees can easily exceed the potential recovery cells."

⚠️ Credit Management Invisible Error Encyclopedia (AAT Core Exam Traps)

  • The Factoring Recourse Risk Flaw: A common conceptual exam trap is assuming that "With Recourse" factoring completely removes bad debt risk from the company's financial sheets. If an injected customer ledger account faults, a factor under recourse terms will charge the bad debt directly back to the company.
  • The DSO Denominator Mistake: When calculating Debtor Days or Days Sales Outstanding, candidates often use Total Revenue instead of **Gross Credit Sales** inside the denominator slot. Cash sales do not generate receivables and must be completely omitted from DSO calculations.
  • The Premature Litigation Trap: Recommending instant legal action or court collection filings the first day an invoice crosses its payment terms is a fatal case study error. Credit control commands a **progressive collection pathway** (statements, reminder alerts, phone calls, final demand notice letters) before deploying litigation.
